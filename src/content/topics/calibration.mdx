There are two separate questions you can ask of a rating system, and confusing them
causes most of the arguments.

The first is **discrimination**: does it put the riskier obligors in the riskier
grades? That is a question about ordering, and Gini or AUC answers it.

The second is **calibration**: is grade 4 actually a 1.2% chance of default? That is
a question about level, and a model can be excellent at the first while being badly
wrong at the second. A rating system that ranks flawlessly but assigns PDs half the
size of reality produces an ordered, confident, and materially undercapitalised book.

Calibration is the machinery that fixes the level.

## Why the long-run average, and not something else

The instinct is to use recent data — it reflects the portfolio as it is now. The
guidelines push hard the other way, and the reason is visible in the spread above.

A one-year default rate is a draw from a distribution whose mean moves with the
economy. Calibrate on 2019 and you anchor to a benign year. Calibrate on 2020 and you
anchor to a crisis. Neither is the long-run behaviour of the portfolio; both are a
single sample from a cycle that has not finished.

The long-run average default rate is the attempt to average the cycle out. This is
what makes an IRB PD closer to **through-the-cycle** than **point-in-time** — not a
modelling preference, but a direct consequence of anchoring to a multi-year mean.

It also explains why the length and composition of the observation period gets so
much scrutiny. An observation period containing no downturn does not produce a
long-run average; it produces a benign-period average wearing the right label. That
is a data problem no amount of statistical sophistication repairs, and it is
precisely the situation the margin of conservatism exists to cover.

## Central tendency is a constraint, not an output

The fitted curve above is a reasonable description of the grades. It is not yet a
calibration, because nothing has forced it to reproduce the portfolio's own default
experience.

The central tendency is that constraint: the long-run average default rate of the
calibration segment as a whole. The requirement is that the obligor-weighted mean of
the assigned PDs equals it. In the figure the fitted curve gives 2.116% against an
observed 2.145%, so every grade is scaled by 1.0138.

Here the adjustment is small, which is the healthy case — it means the fit was
already broadly consistent with the portfolio. A large anchor scalar is a signal
worth chasing down rather than applying quietly: it usually means the grade
distribution has shifted, the segment is not homogeneous, or the fit is fighting the
data somewhere.

Note the direction of the logic. You do not fit a curve and report whatever average
falls out. You fit a curve and then **force** its average to the observed one. The
central tendency has authority over the fit, not the other way around.

## The margin of conservatism is deliberate bias

The last step adds 10%, and it is worth being precise about what that number is.

It is not a safety factor sprinkled on for comfort, and it is not an error bar. It is
a deliberate upward bias applied because the estimate is known to be uncertain, and
because the consequences of being too low are asymmetric. EBA/GL/2017/16 organises it
into categories — data and methodological deficiencies, changes in underwriting or
recovery processes, and general estimation error — and expects each to be justified
and quantified separately rather than bundled into one round number.

The consequence is that a calibrated PD is *not* a best estimate. It is a best
estimate that has been pushed upward on purpose. Anyone comparing IRB PDs against
realised default rates and finding them consistently too high has not found a bug —
they have found the margin of conservatism working as designed.

## Why the level matters more than it looks

The [supervisory formula](/G-IRB/topics/supervisory-formula) is steepest at low PD.
Moving grade 3 from 0.39% to 0.46% — the difference between the long-run average and
the calibrated PD in the table above — is a small absolute change and a large
relative one, and the risk-weight curve amplifies exactly that kind of move.

This is why calibration disputes are never academic. Discrimination arguments change
which obligors sit in which grade. Calibration arguments change the capital
requirement of the entire book at once, and they do it hardest in the good grades
where most of the exposure usually lives.
