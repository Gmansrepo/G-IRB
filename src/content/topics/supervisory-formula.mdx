Almost every discussion about an IRB model ends at the same place: a risk weight.
Whatever effort went into the rating system, the calibration, the downturn LGD
analysis, the output is three numbers — PD, LGD, M — pushed through one closed-form
expression laid down in CRR Article 153.

It is worth knowing that expression well, because it decides which modelling
decisions actually matter and which ones the formula quietly flattens away.

## The model underneath

The formula is not an empirical fit. It is the closed-form solution to a specific
model — the asymptotic single risk factor (ASRF) model, built on Vasicek's
extension of Merton. Three assumptions get you there:

1. **One systematic factor.** Every borrower's fortunes depend on a single common
   driver plus idiosyncratic noise. Call the common driver the economy.
2. **Infinite granularity.** The portfolio holds infinitely many infinitesimally
   small exposures, so idiosyncratic risk diversifies away completely and only the
   systematic component survives.
3. **Portfolio invariance.** As a consequence, the capital for an exposure depends
   only on that exposure's own characteristics — never on what else is in the book.

The third one is the reason the framework is usable at all. Capital becomes additive:
each exposure carries its own charge, and the portfolio total is just the sum. No
covariance matrix, no portfolio optimisation, no bank-specific concentration model.

It is also the assumption that is plainly false. A real portfolio is not infinitely
granular and rarely rests on a single factor. That gap is exactly what Pillar 2 is
for — concentration risk is not missing from the framework by oversight, it was
deliberately pushed outside the Pillar 1 formula.

## Reading the terms

**The inner `N(·)` is a conditional default probability.** Take the PD, restate it as
a threshold on a standard normal scale with `G(PD)`, then ask: if the systematic
factor comes in at its 99.9th-percentile worst outcome, what share of these borrowers
falls below the threshold? That share is the default rate in a one-in-a-thousand year.

**`G(0.999)` is a supervisory choice, not a model output.** It is fixed at 3.09 and
corresponds to a 99.9% confidence level over a one-year horizon — roughly, a
solvency standard where the bank survives all but a one-in-a-thousand year. Nothing
in the maths requires 0.999; it was set by supervisors and does not move.

**Subtracting `PD · LGD` removes expected loss.** Capital covers *unexpected* loss
only. Expected loss is meant to be covered by provisions, which is why the two
frameworks — IRB capital and IFRS 9 impairment — have to be read together but never
add up cleanly.

**`R` is where the exposure class lives.** For corporates, correlation falls from
0.24 to 0.12 as PD rises. The intuition: a borrower already close to default is
driven more by its own circumstances than by the economy, so its defaults are less
synchronised with everyone else's. Toggle the overlay above and the entire difference
between a mortgage and a revolving retail exposure is visible as a gap between two
curves — same LGD, different correlation assumption.

**`MA` stretches the charge for longer exposures**, and it is normalised so that
`MA = 1` at one year, not at 2.5. This trips people up: at the standard corporate
assumption of M = 2.5 years the adjustment is already about 1.26, not 1. Retail
carries no maturity adjustment at all.

## What this means in practice

Move the PD slider across its range and watch the risk weight. Two things stand out.

The curve is **steepest at low PD**. Going from 0.1% to 0.3% roughly doubles the
risk weight; going from 10% to 30% barely moves it. For a good-quality portfolio,
small changes in calibration translate into large changes in capital — which is why
calibration disputes are rarely academic, and why the margin of conservatism gets
the scrutiny it does.

**LGD scales the whole thing almost linearly**, while PD does not. Halving LGD
halves the risk weight at every PD. That asymmetry is worth remembering when
deciding where to spend model development effort: an LGD assumption applies its
error uniformly, whereas a PD error is amplified or damped depending on where you
sit on the curve.
